How to do a dead bug

Press your lower back into the floor. Move slowly.

With coach Sarah

Works
Deep core
Beginners start with
6 reps
Equipment
None

Step by step

  1. Lie on your back Arms up to the ceiling, knees bent above your hips.
  2. Lower back down Press it gently into the floor and keep it there.
  3. Opposite arm and leg Reach one arm back as the other leg straightens.
  4. Slow is the point Return to the middle, then switch sides.

Common mistakes

  • Your lower back lifting off the floor. Press it gently into the floor and keep it there. Only lower your arm and leg as far as you can without your back arching.
  • Moving fast. Take about three seconds to reach out and three to come back. Speed hides the work.
  • Same arm, same leg. It's the opposite arm and leg: right arm with left leg, then switch.
  • Holding your breath. Breathe out as you reach. It helps your belly stay tight and your back stay down.
  • Lifting your head. Let your head rest on the floor so your neck stays relaxed.

Muscles it works and why it matters

The dead bug works your deep core, the muscles that wrap around your middle like a corset. It teaches the most important core skill there is: keeping your middle steady while your arms and legs move. That control is what makes planks, push-ups and lunges feel solid later on, which is why it's the first rung of Tonestep's core ladder.

How many and how often

Start with 6 reps on each side, two or three sets, two or three days a week. To make it easier, keep your knee bent and just tap your heel to the floor. When 6 a side feel easy with your back staying down, straighten your leg more as it lowers, or move up to the bird dog.

Who it's good for, and when to go easier

It's one of the best first core moves: no weight on your wrists or knees, no balance needed, and you can feel when you're doing it right. If your lower back aches, make the movements smaller and slower, and see a doctor or physical therapist if it doesn't settle.

The dead bug is done on your back. Many pregnancy guidelines suggest avoiding long stretches lying flat as pregnancy goes on, so if you're pregnant, ask your doctor or midwife what's right for you. After a baby, a pelvic health physical therapist can tell you when core work like this is a good idea.
