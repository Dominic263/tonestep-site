How to do a push-up

Squeeze your glutes so your body stays long.

With coach Sarah

Works
Chest, triceps, core
Beginners start with
6 reps
Equipment
None

Step by step

  1. High plank Hands under your shoulders, legs long, up on your toes.
  2. Squeeze everything Glutes and belly tight, body like a board.
  3. Lower as one piece Chest and hips go down together.
  4. Push the floor away All the way back to straight arms.

Common mistakes

  • Hips sagging toward the floor. The work slides out of your chest and into your lower back. Squeeze your glutes and brace your belly before the first rep, and keep them squeezed until the last. If your hips still drop, spend a few more sessions on knee push-ups.
  • Hips piking up. It feels easier because your arms carry less of you. Picture one straight board from your head to your heels. A ten-second video from the side shows you the truth quickly.
  • Elbows flared straight out. Elbows out wide like a T put your shoulders in an awkward spot. Let them travel back at an angle, closer to an arrow than a T.
  • Half reps. Lower until your chest is close to the floor, then press all the way back to straight arms. Four full reps beat ten half ones.
  • Chin first. If your head dives toward the floor before your chest, look at the floor just ahead of your hands and keep your neck long. Chest and hips arrive together.

Muscles it works and why it matters

A push-up works your chest, the backs of your arms (your triceps) and the fronts of your shoulders, while your core holds everything in a straight line. It's a plank that moves. That's why it's such a good single move for upper-body strength: it's the same push you use to get yourself up off the floor, shove a heavy door or lift a suitcase into the trunk.

How many and how often

Start with 6 reps with good form, two or three sets, resting about a minute between them. Train two or three days a week with a rest day in between; muscles get stronger while they recover. If you can't reach 6 yet, do as many full push-ups as you can, then finish the set on your knees. That still counts.

When you can do every rep with good form two sessions in a row, add a rep or two, or slow the way down to a count of three. The next rung up is the pike push-up, which moves more of the work into your shoulders.

Who it's good for, and when to go easier

The full push-up is for anyone who can already do clean knee push-ups. If it's not there yet, there's no shame in the ladder: wall push-ups and incline push-ups train the same pattern with less weight.

Push-ups put weight through your hands with your wrists bent back. If your wrists ache, go back to the wall, which asks far less of them, and tell Tonestep when you set up your plan: it serves fewer moves with weight on your hands. Pain in a wrist, elbow or shoulder that lingers after training is worth showing a doctor or physical therapist. If you're pregnant or have recently had a baby, check with your doctor or midwife before you start, and ask about push-ups and planks in particular.
