How to do a bird dog

Reach long. Keep your hips level.

With coach Lena

Works
Core, lower back
Beginners start with
6 reps
Equipment
None

Step by step

  1. On all fours Hands under shoulders, knees under hips, back flat.
  2. Reach long One arm forward, the opposite leg back.
  3. Keep your hips level Like balancing a cup of tea on your back.
  4. Bring them back in Then switch sides, nice and slow.

Common mistakes

  • Hips tipping when your leg lifts. Imagine balancing a cup of tea on your lower back. Lift your leg only as high as your hip, and keep both hips facing the floor.
  • Reaching up instead of long. Lifting your arm or leg high arches your back. Reach away from your body, as if someone were gently pulling your hand and heel apart.
  • Rushing. Hold the long reach for a slow count of two, then bring your elbow and knee back in. Slow is the whole point.
  • Dropping your head or craning your neck. Look at the floor just ahead of your hands so your neck stays in line with your back.
  • Sinking into your supporting shoulder. Push the floor away with the hand that stays down.

Muscles it works and why it matters

The bird dog works your deep core and the muscles along your lower back, with your glutes and shoulders joining in. It trains your middle to stay still while your arms and legs move, which is what it has to do every time you walk, reach or carry something. It's gentle, but done slowly it's harder than it looks.

How many and how often

Start with 6 reps on each side, two or three sets, two or three days a week. It also makes a good warm-up before other core work. When 6 a side feel steady and your hips stay level, hold each reach a little longer. The next rung up is the plank; if the bird dog is too much today, start with the dead bug.

Who it's good for, and when to go easier

It's a good early core move for beginners, and a calm one for days you want to move without much effort. You're on your hands and knees, so a folded towel under your knees helps. If your wrists complain, the dead bug trains the same idea lying on your back, with no weight on your hands.

Back pain that comes on during the move, or stays afterward, is a reason to stop and see a doctor or physical therapist. If you're pregnant or recently had a baby, ask your doctor or midwife before starting new core exercises.
