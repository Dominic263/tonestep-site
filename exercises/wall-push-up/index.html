How to do a wall push-up

Lean in as one straight line, chest to the wall.

With coach Sarah

Works
Chest, shoulders, triceps
Beginners start with
10 reps
Equipment
A wall or sturdy surface

Step by step

  1. Face a wall Stand an arm's length away, palms flat at chest height.
  2. Lean in as one line Head to heels in a straight line, heels down.
  3. Bend your elbows Bring your chest close to the wall.
  4. Press the wall away Back to straight arms, body stays straight.

Common mistakes

  • Standing too close. With your feet near the wall there's almost nothing to push. Stand an arm's length away. The further back your feet, the harder it gets.
  • Bending in the middle. Hips that stick out or sag toward the wall turn it into a nod, not a push-up. Squeeze your glutes and keep one straight line from your head to your heels.
  • Hands too high or too wide. Hands above your head shift the work into your shoulders. Keep your palms flat at chest height, a little wider than your shoulders.
  • Heels popping up. Keep your heels down. If they lift as you lean in, step a little closer until they stay.
  • Rushing. Count two on the way in and one on the way out. Slow reps make an easy angle much more useful.

Muscles it works and why it matters

The wall push-up works your chest, shoulders and the backs of your arms, the same muscles as a floor push-up, with a fraction of the load. It teaches the whole pattern: one straight body, elbows bending, pressing the surface away. Learn it here and every rung above it feels familiar. It's also a gentle way to wake up your upper body before a harder workout.

How many and how often

Start with 10 reps, two or three sets, two or three days a week with a rest day in between. When all 10 feel easy and your body stays straight for two sessions in a row, step your feet back a little, or move up to the incline push-up on a counter, a sturdy table or the arm of a couch. Some women spend a week here, some a few sessions. There's no rush: the wall is a real step, not a warm-up you have to apologize for.

Who it's good for, and when to go easier

It suits complete beginners, anyone coming back after a long break, and anyone who wants push-up practice without getting down on the floor. Because you stand, your wrists bend less and carry far less weight than on the floor, so it's a good place to stay while wrists or knees need a rest.

If a shoulder complains, bring your hands a little lower and make the movement smaller. Pain that sticks around is worth showing a doctor or physical therapist. If you're pregnant or recovering from birth, check with your doctor or midwife before starting any new exercise, this one included.
